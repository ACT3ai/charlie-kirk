On **September 2–3, 2026**, **[Stew Peters](/Influencers/podcasts-stew-peters)** posted that a Google Trends result
had been erased while it was being discussed on a live broadcast:

> "The search results showing that Butch Hibbs was Google image-searched from Fort Huachuca on 9-7-25 were wiped in
> real time while @AnaEscobarShow was discussing them live on-air."

The chart in question is the one this site already covers at
**[Butch Hibbs — Sierra Vista Trends Spike](/GoogleSearches/butch-hibbs-sierra-vista-trends)**: a Google Trends
Explore query for the term `"Burton Hibbs Calvalry church"`, restricted to the **Tucson (Sierra Vista) AZ** metro,
**Image Search**, January 1 to September 9, 2025, which showed a flat line at zero for eight months and a single
spike to 100 on **September 7, 2025**.

**Peters's sentence and the underlying screenshot are two different claims, and only the narrower one is supported.**
Google Trends reports metro-wide interest in a typed string. It cannot show that a search came from a base, from a
government network, or from any particular person, and it says nothing at all about where the person whose name was
typed actually was. The chart never placed Butch Hibbs at Fort Huachuca, and neither does this page.

## What the video shows

The segment posted by **Ana Escobar** runs 1 minute 24 seconds and is a live screen share. She is mid-sentence about
using spelling variants as a way to "separate signal from noise" when she pulls the query back up and finds it empty.
Her reaction, in her own words on air:

> "Well, please. I was like, is it gone? Oh my gosh, you guys. It's gone. You're kidding. Okay, hold on … There's no
> way. Okay, let me add it to the stage."

> "Guys, you're joking. You're literally kidding. Okay, so see that? See how there's nothing, right? I'm like, did I
> misspell it? I might have. What if I misspelled it? What if it's something else? They've been wiping them past."

> "So you guys right now, you're looking at a live look at Google Trends, right? You see this, Burton Hibbs, cavalry,
> right? You see how it's misspelled the cavalry, right? … So to me, I thought that that information was a good way to
> separate signal from noise. When I'm going through and I'm trying to figure out, you know, Burton Hibbs — well, not
> many people call him Burton."

Two things are worth noting about her own handling of it. First, she raises the misspelling possibility herself, on
air, before anyone else does — she asks whether she typed the term wrong. Second, the reason the misspelled term
mattered to her in the first place is that `"Calvalry"` is an unusual error; a term almost nobody would type by
accident is a term whose spike is harder to write off as background noise. That same property is what makes it fragile:
a low-volume string sits right at the threshold where Google stops reporting a curve at all.

## What the screenshot adds

Peters also published an iPhone screenshot of the direct-message thread in which Escobar reported it to him. It carries
three lines and one embedded frame:

* **"Stew they wiped the Burton Hibbs search"**
* **"It happened while I was live on air"**
* **"And I tried both with caps and without"**

The embedded frame shows the Google Trends Explore page with the same search term, region and filters, and the
"Interest over time" panel replaced by Google's standard notice: **"Hmm, your search doesn't have enough data to show
here."**

The third line is the only control the pair performed, and it is worth stating plainly what it does and does not rule
out. Trying the query with and without capitals eliminates a case-sensitivity mistake by the operator. **It does not
distinguish a removal from the ordinary behaviour of a very-low-volume term**, because both produce the identical
"not enough data" panel.

## What would settle it, and what nobody has

This site has already had to correct itself once for reading a normal platform behaviour as a suppression event, and
the discipline that came out of that applies directly here: **never call something a removal until a control has failed
the same way.** Three checks would move this from an anecdote to a finding, and none of them appear in the material
published so far:

1. **A control term.** Another string of comparable rarity, in the same metro, on the same filters, at the same moment.
   If it also returns "not enough data", the panel is a volume threshold, not a deletion.
2. **An archived before-state.** A dated capture of the working chart from an independent archive — not a screenshot
   held by the person making the claim. The [Trends deletion](/GoogleSearches/trends-deletion-suppression) page records
   that this is the recurring gap in every claim of this type on this site.
3. **A re-pull after the fact.** Trends results for thin terms fluctuate as Google re-samples. A term that returns to
   normal on its own was never removed. The one claim in the [Google Searches](/GoogleSearches/overview) section that
   has been shown to [reproduce on demand](/GoogleSearches/nester-israel-trends-repull) is the exception, not the rule.

What genuinely is unusual is the **timing** — the panel changed during the minutes the term was being displayed to an
audience. That is either a coincidence, or it is not, and a coincidence of that shape is exactly what a control test
exists to measure. **Until one is run, this page records an on-camera observation, not a proven act of suppression.**

## Why this sits in the cover-up section

The value of preserving it is not that it proves anything. It is that this investigation's standing complaint about
Trends claims is that the before-state is always gone by the time anyone looks. Here the before-state was captured — as
a screenshot published the previous day — and the after-state was captured on video. **That is the closest thing to a
paired record any Trends claim on this site has produced**, whatever it eventually turns out to mean.
