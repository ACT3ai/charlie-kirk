[Baron Coleman](/Influencers/podcasts-baron) — a former attorney and one of the most-followed
independent investigators on this case — appeared on Episode 458 of the *Julian Dorey Podcast*
and was asked whether he had spoken with sources close to [Tyler Robinson](/People/tyler-robinson)'s
family. His answer, as he reports it, cuts directly against the story the public has been told
about a confession to the parents.

Everything on this page is Coleman's reported account of private conversations and text messages
he says he has seen. None of it is a court finding, and none of it has been confirmed by the
family on the record. It is presented as one investigator's claim, offered to the public debate.

## The family does not believe he did it

Asked whether he had spoken with anyone connected to the family's thinking, Coleman answered
plainly: *"Yes. They don't believe he did it."* Asked to confirm he meant the mother and father,
he pointed to a second channel as well:

> "There's a guy on X. I know him, I believe him. I have personal knowledge that he has spoken
> to the grandmother and that's well known. His grandfather's gun, right? That was the alleged
> thing. … And he has spoken to the grandmother and I've seen text messages between the family.
> They don't believe he did it."

Coleman then lists what he says the family denies:

> "They deny that he confessed to them. They deny that he turned himself in because he was
> guilty. They don't think he did it. Now, did they think he did it at the time? No. They
> never thought he did it. They never saw it."

The [grandfather's rifle](./Weapon_Rifle) referenced here is the weapon at the center of the
state's ballistics case, and the [surrender](./Surrender) is the other pillar Coleman says the
family disputes the meaning of.

## The parents were not on the witness list

Coleman's sharpest point is procedural rather than anecdotal — and it is checkable against the
public record of the hearing:

> "What's miraculous is that we get to the preliminary hearing. We've been told for 10 months
> by [TPUSA](/TPUSA/overview) people, by their spokespeople, by their hangers on, their paid
> contributors — we've been told for 10 months that he confessed to his parents, and that's a
> key component of this. That's why we know he did it. He confessed to his parents. He turned
> himself in. … We get to the preliminary hearing. They're not even on the witness list."

His argument is that a parental confession, if it existed, would be the single strongest piece
of evidence a prosecutor could put on:

> "You talk about bulletproof probable cause. If your mom gets on the stand and says, yeah, he
> told me he did it — yeah, you're fine. It's over. We don't need a trial. I mean, at that
> point, I'm going: well, maybe I'm wrong this whole time."

## The devil's-advocate objection, and his answer

Julian Dorey pressed the obvious counterargument — that parents may simply be in denial:

> "Could part of it be like, there's no way my baby did that? And it's just setting in after
> enough time to think about it being like, you know what? I just don't believe it."

Coleman's response is that a denial made to investigators leaves a paper trail either way:

> "If his parents told law enforcement that he did it, that would be documented. If you put
> them on the stand and they deny that he said it, you impeach them with the documentation. …
> He didn't tell them. I'm telling you, that is so much evidence that they've been lying to us
> this whole year. Otherwise, we would have heard from him."

Readers should weigh the counterpoint fairly. A prosecutor may keep family members off a
preliminary-hearing witness list for reasons unrelated to the strength of their testimony — the
hearing has a lower evidentiary bar than trial, and the state may prefer to hold witnesses back.
Coleman's claim about the text messages he says he has seen is, at present, unverified by any
public document. No member of the Robinson family has confirmed these statements on the record.

## Why it matters

The reported confession to the parents has been one of the two load-bearing planks of the public
case against Tyler Robinson — the other being that he
[turned himself in because he was guilty](./Surrender). This site's other pages document why
[citizen investigators dispute the shooter identification](/Tyler_Robinson_Not_Assassin/overview)
and where the [family](./Family) sits in the record. If Coleman's account of the family's
position is accurate, both planks are weaker than they have been presented.
