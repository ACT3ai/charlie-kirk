## Audience

Who is looking at this. What they already believe, what they already know, and
what they are scanning for. On the Planes pages this is usually a reader who has
seen a tail number in an X post and arrived with either "this proves shadowing"
or "this was debunked" already in their head. Write for both.

## What they should learn

The takeaway, stated as the ONE SENTENCE the reader should be able to say out
loud after four seconds of looking.

## Conceptual framing

The metaphor, the structure, the shape of the argument the picture makes.

## What we are educating on

The actual content being taught.

## Perspective

What the reader gets perspective ON — what they currently cannot see that this
image makes visible.

## Polarity and scope

Are we zooming IN on one detail or widening OUT to show scale? Often both, and
the tension between the two is the graphic. Say how the zoom level is expressed
visually.

## Numbers

Every number that appears, what it means, and how it is communicated (a big
numeral, a count of repeated marks, a bar, a ratio). Name the numbers we
deliberately leave out, and why.

EVIDENCE RULE FOR PLANES INFOGRAPHICS. Every number on a Planes infographic must
be traceable to a file in this repo — `overlaps.csv`, `flights.csv`,
`tpusa_events.csv`, `aircraft_costs.csv`, or a recovered trace under
`site/docs/Planes/{TAIL}/data/recovered/`. Say which file each number came from.

## Sizing

What is bigger and what is smaller, and why. Size carries importance; state the
importance ranking that drives it.

## Framing and placement

What is frame left, frame right, higher, lower. What sits inside a frame/box/card
and what is unframed and bleeds. If there is a timeline, say explicitly how time
is expressed — left-to-right axis, stacked bands, a spiral, converging lines.

## Screen percentages

Roughly what share of the frame each concept takes. Assign these and reconcile
them against the frame-left / frame-right decision above. General guidance, not
a pixel constraint.

## Order of understanding

A NUMBERED list, most important first.

1. Introduces the concept and the issue the reader should be trying to understand.
2. The first thing they should actually understand.
3. ...
4. ...
5. ...

This ordering drives the sizing and placement above: higher priority gets larger
and lands where the eye goes first.

## On-image text

Every word that appears in the image, written out in its FINAL wording — not a
description of the wording. The title across the top, the subline, section
labels, callouts, the source line. People scan; the title alone has to tell them
what this is about.

## What this image must NOT claim

Required section on this site. A trace proves presence, never purpose, and never
occupancy — it places nobody aboard. An absence proves that a volunteer network
heard nothing, not that an aircraft was elsewhere. Name here, explicitly, the
claim a careless reader might take from the picture that the data does not
support, and say how the layout or a caption prevents it.

## Model-generated vs plotted

Say which this is:

* PLOTTED — rendered by a script straight out of a CSV. Every mark is real. This
  is the only kind that may be published as a chart with numbers in it.
* MODEL-GENERATED — rendered by NanoBanana / GPT Imagine from the prompt. A
  generative model draws a PLAUSIBLE chart, it does not plot a real one. Fine for
  a conceptual or diagrammatic graphic; never for one a reader would read values
  off. See the Overlap_Timeline case in `site/docs/Planes/CLAUDE.md`.
