# Every United States Flight By The Egyptian Jets, 2022–2025

**Published 16 September 2026.**

Every count on this site about these aircraft has had the same hole in it. The claims are built
from dates somebody already pointed at — this jet, at this field, on this day — and a check of
those dates can only ever confirm or refute that list. It cannot say **how often these aircraft
were in the United States at all**, which is the number every "N overlaps" figure is silently
measured against.

**That number now exists.** We bought FlightAware's record of the five Egyptian-registered
aircraft a week at a time, across four calendar years, whether or not anybody had claimed
anything about that week: **1,025 seven-day windows, 1,021 flights, and 193 of them touching the
United States.** Then we bought the flown path of every one of those 193 — **92,098 recorded
positions** — so the route across the country is on the record, not just the two ends of it.

**None of it places any person aboard any aircraft.** A flight record shows where an aircraft
went. It shows nothing about who was on it, or why it went.

**The two aircraft that flew to the United States most are not the two the public claims are
about.** SU-BTV and SU-BTU account for 115 of the 193 United States legs between them. SU-BTT,
the "yellow plane" that carries most of the attention, made 46. SU-BND, the "blue plane", made
20 — fewer than any of them except SU-BGM, despite flying the most of any of the five overall.

**United States legs by year:** 35 in 2022, 60 in 2023, 34 in 2024, 61 in 2025. The traffic did
not build towards September 2025. It rose, fell and rose again.

**Thirteen airports in four years.** Not a fleet ranging across the country — a short, repeated
circuit.

**The pattern changes shape after 2023, and nothing in the per-claim checks could have shown it.**
St Louis carried 45 visits across 2022 and 2023 and then **stops completely**. Provo, Lincoln,
Omaha and Minot carry **nothing at all** until 2024 and then carry 74 visits between them. The
fleet did not simply keep doing what it had been doing; where it went changed.

**And one absence is worth more than any of the arrivals.** [Phoenix](/Planes/Airports/KPHX) is
where Turning Point USA is based, and it carries more sourced Charlie Kirk and TPUSA events than
any other field in this investigation. **In four years, none of these five aircraft landed
there once.**

legs from that one field straight to an Egyptian air force base.

## One day in four years

**On exactly one date — 14 July 2023 — were two of these five aircraft in the United States at the
same time** ([SU-BGM](/Planes/SU-BGM/overview) and [SU-BTU](/Planes/SU-BTU/overview)).

On every other United States day in four years there was **one** of them in the country. Whatever
this fleet is doing, it rotates through: one aircraft at a time, on a circuit of thirteen fields.
A reader picturing a formation of Egyptian jets crossing America should put that picture down.

## The paths, not just the endpoints

A flight record says an aircraft went from one airport to another. It does not say what it flew
over. So the flown track of every United States leg was bought separately.

* **193 of 193 United States legs have their path on record.** 92,098 individual positions.
* **Three came back with no positions at all** — the record of the flight exists, the track does
  not. They are counted and named rather than quietly dropped.
* Typical cruise height is **43,000 ft**, which is above ordinary airline traffic.
* The oldest path we hold is [SU-BTT](/Planes/SU-BTT/overview)'s **13 November 2022 flight from
  Paris to [Wichita](/Planes/Airports/KICT)** — 666 recorded positions, bought four years after
  it flew.

The positions themselves are FlightAware's data and are held privately. What is published is our
summary of each path: how many positions, the highest altitude, and when the track starts and
ends. Those columns are in `flightaware_derived/us_flights.csv` in this repository.

## What this does not show

* **193 United States legs is not 193 overlaps with anybody.** An overlap needs a person, a place
  and a date on the other side of it. This page is one half of that pairing, and the Kirk half —
  particularly [Erika Kirk's](/Planes/Erika-Flight-Logs-Erased) — is the half this investigation
  has never been able to source properly.
* **It shows no purpose and no occupancy.** Not who was aboard, not who paid, not why.
* **These are FlightAware's records**, built from filed flight plans and air traffic data. The
  volunteer ADS-B archives this site also holds are a different measurement, and where the two
  disagree both are published.
* **A week with no flights is not proof the aircraft sat still.** It is proof FlightAware holds
  nothing for that week.
* **These five aircraft are not hidden.** FlightAware's block list was checked for each of them
  and none is on it — unlike [N102DZ, N582MM, N888KG and T7-ELL](/Planes/Deleted-Flight-Records),
  whose flights the tracking sites withhold. For the Egyptian tails, an empty week really is an
  empty week.

## The innocent explanations, on the record

Every field on that list has an ordinary reason a business jet would use it, and those reasons
are in this repository's own airport table:

* **Lincoln, Nebraska** is the **headquarters and main plant of Duncan Aviation**, one of the
  largest business-jet maintenance operations in the world. **Provo, Utah** is Duncan's other
  full-service facility. Between them those two fields carry 44 of the visits counted above.
* **Wichita** has **Yingling Aviation**, an FAA Part 145 repair station that services Dassault
  Falcons — the type most of this fleet flies.
* **Wilmington, Delaware** is a long-established East Coast customs and fuel stop, and
  **Bangor, Maine** is the classic transatlantic technical stop.
* **Omaha, Minot and Detroit** are customs ports of entry — which is what an aircraft arriving
  from Europe needs.

A maintenance visit and a surveillance flight look identical in a flight record. **This page
cannot tell them apart, and does not claim to.**

## How this was made, and how to check it

* **Source:** FlightAware AeroAPI v4 flight history, by registration, in 7-day windows — the
  largest span the interface allows. 1 January 2022 to 1 January 2026.
* **1,025 windows bought, every one answered, none truncated.** Where a week held more flights
  than a single page returns, the tool says so rather than silently cutting the list.
* **Cost:** $20.50 for the flight records, $2.32 for the 193 paths.
* **The raw responses are not published** — FlightAware's terms do not allow it. Every one is
  restated fact by fact in `flightaware_restated/`, and a checker rebuilds each restated file
  from the raw response and compares it field by field. All 1,114 pass.
* **The derived tables on this page** are in `flightaware_derived/`: `us_flights.csv`,
  `flights_by_tail_year.csv` and `us_airports_by_year.csv`.
* **The tools** are `aeroapi_fleet_sweep.js` (the calendar sweep), `aeroapi_fleet_tracks.js`
  (the paths) and `build_fleet_us_tables.py` (these tables), in
  [the proprietary API lane](/Planes/following/apis/proprietary/knowledge).
