# Overlap Recovery

The [73-overlap claim](/Planes/following/73_overlaps) is a spreadsheet. Each row says: on **this
date**, **this Egyptian government jet** was at **this American city**, where a Kirk was. Most of
those rows have never had a primary flight record behind them — they rest on a screenshot of a
tracking site, or on somebody's summary of one.

:::danger[The number on this page was corrected on 26 August 2026]

This site published **24 confirmed · 12 refuted** for two days. Reading every position fix in every
recovered trace shows **9 of those 12 refutations were wrong** — six were scored against the wrong
day, and two were scored off the shorter of two archives while the longer one showed the aircraft
**at the airport it was said to be 1,853 km from**. The corrected result is **25 at the claimed field ·
1 right metro · 3 refuted** of the 29 rows primary data can decide. The old figures are kept below so the
correction is visible rather than quietly edited away.

:::

Every row that can be reduced to an aircraft and a date was pulled directly from the raw ADS-B
archives. **69 distinct (aircraft, date) pairs**, each one asked of **both** free daily archives —
`globe.airplanes.live` and `adsb.lol` — on the alleged day **and the UTC day either side**, because
an evening departure in the United States lands on tomorrow's date in UTC.

| | |
|---|---|
| Alleged (aircraft, date) pairs tested | **69** |
| A real ADS-B track exists **on the alleged day** | **23** |
| A track exists **only on a neighbouring day** | **7** |
| Neither free archive holds anything in the three-day window | **39** |
| Of the 23: the track puts the aircraft **in the alleged country** | **21** |
| Of the 23: the track puts it **somewhere else entirely** | **2** |

Two aircraft account for the whole set: **[SU-BTT](/Planes/SU-BTT/overview)** (Dassault Falcon 7X,
hex `0101D3`) appears in 53 of the 69 pairs, and **[SU-BND](/Planes/SU-BND/overview)** (Gulfstream,
hex `01003E`) in the other 16.

## Two later runs have since been done — and the third one is the number to quote

**Read this before quoting any number above.** Three passes have now been made over this
spreadsheet, each one asking a harder question of more data than the last. The third pass read
**every position fix in every recovered trace**, and it found that the second pass — the
**24 confirmed · 12 refuted** run that this site published for two days — was wrong in a specific,
enumerable way. **Nine of its twelve refutations do not survive contact with the raw files.**

defects were found, each one traced to named rows:

|---|---|---|

| **Half a row refuted as a whole row** | OWENS-015 claims "St. Louis then Goose Bay". The trace **confirms the St. Louis departure** and is silent on Goose Bay. It was scored as refuted. | OWENS-015 |

### The single hardest reversal

**OWENS-024 / SITE-003 — SU-BTT, 3 April 2024, claimed Wichita (KICT).** This site published
*"Refuted. Its closest approach to KICT was 1,852.96 km. Both archives agree."*

The airplanes.live trace for that day **opens with the aircraft climbing out of Wichita** —
13:43:30 UTC, **1.4 km from KICT at 342 ft above the field** — and runs nonstop at FL410 to a
landing at Wilmington, Delaware at 16:05 UTC. The adsb.lol trace does not begin until 14:15 UTC,
already over Missouri, *after the aircraft had left Kansas*. The published 1,853 km figure was the
closest approach of the shorter trace only.

**The claim was correct and this site called it false.** It is now scored
`CONFIRMS_DEPARTURE_FROM_CLAIMED_FIELD`.

### And the result is still binary, which settles a separate argument entirely

Two tests are argued in public about this spreadsheet: the sceptics' **strict** rule — same ICAO
airport, plus or minus three days — and the sheet's own looser stated tolerance of **50 to 100
miles**. Which rule you adopt has been treated as deciding the outcome. Every decidable row was
banded by measured distance to the field it names:

| Band | Rows |
|---|---|
| within 15 km of the claimed field | **25** |
| 15–80 km — inside the sheet's own tolerance, outside a same-airport test | **1** (OWENS-012, coverage ends 27.6 km out on a descent) |
| 80–1,500 km — same region | **1** (OWENS-015, coverage ends 1,063 km short of Goose Bay, tracking towards it) |
| more than 1,500 km away | **2** (OWENS-036, OWENS-050) |

**Twenty-five of the twenty-nine decidable rows pass the strict test and the loose one alike**, and
only two rows in all 85 fall in the band where the choice of rule changes the answer. The argument
about which test to use decides almost nothing.

### Corrections went the other way too — this is not a one-way pass

**OWENS-010 — SU-BTT, 1 April 2023, claimed St. Louis Lambert (KSTL).** The row keeps its
AT_CLAIMED_AIRPORT verdict — 0.4 km is 0.4 km — but the *reading* of it was wrong. That 0.4 km
happened at **3,357 ft above the field**: an overflight. The trace then continues away from Lambert
and ends on final approach to a **different airport**, 2.2 km from **KSUS (Spirit of St. Louis)** at
600 ft. Four independent sources agree fix for fix. The aircraft reached the St. Louis metro; it did
not land at the field the row names. **Distance without altitude is not a landing record.**

That distinction applies to more than one row. **Fifteen of the twenty-five corroborations are
airborne positions inside 15 km and below 4,000 ft above the field** — an arrival, a departure or a
low pass. **Ten carry the on-ground flag.** They are labelled separately row by row rather than
rolled together, because they are not the same strength of evidence.

**SITE-001 — 2 April 2023** was scored as confirmed on a note dated 1 April. **No trace exists for
2 April 2023 for either tail.** Both archives were asked and held nothing. That row is now
**not tested**.

**None of which locates a person.** A row scored "at the claimed field" confirms the **aircraft**
half of the pairing. The Kirk half is unchanged, and for Erika Kirk it is close to empty — see
[Erika Flight Logs Erased](/Planes/Erika-Flight-Logs-Erased).

## What this tests, and what it cannot touch

**This tests the flight leg. It does not test the shadowing.** That distinction is the whole page,
so it is stated before the data rather than after it.

An ADS-B trace establishes that an airframe was in a place at a time. It establishes nothing about
who was aboard it, and nothing at all about where anybody named Kirk was that day. **A recovered
track showing SU-BTT on the ramp at Wichita is not evidence that Charlie or Erika Kirk was in
Wichita.** It is evidence that the aeroplane was.

The overlap claim is a *pairing* — aircraft here, person here, therefore following. Everything on
this page speaks to the first half of that pairing only. The second half still rests on a set of
Kirk locations this repository cannot reproduce, and for Erika Kirk in particular **no dated
itinerary has ever been published**. That gap is untouched by any amount of ADS-B work, and it is
the weakest point in the aircraft angle. See
[Erika Flight Logs Erased](/Planes/Erika-Flight-Logs-Erased).

Two further limits carry through every line below:

* **A nearest-airport label is geometry, not a landing record.** The label is simply the closest
  known field to the first and last position in the trace. The distance is printed beside it every
  time so the reader can judge it. A 1.4 km label is an aircraft on the airport; a 40 km label is an
  aircraft that was still airborne when the last receiver lost it.
* **An absent track is an absent track.** It means a volunteer receiver network heard nothing. It
  does **not** mean the aircraft did not fly, and it does **not** mean a transponder was switched
  off. Both free daily archives begin in 2023, so a 2022 miss is guaranteed by construction and
  proves nothing whatever.

**Read the distances.** Fourteen of these twenty-three tracks end within **two kilometres** of a
known airport — that is an aircraft on the field, not an aircraft in the general vicinity. In several
the field is the one the spreadsheet names: SU-BTT into Wilmington at **1.63 km** on 6 April 2023,

Read the shape of the routes too, because it is the same one over and over: **Paris-Le Bourget to an
American field, then American field to American field, then back east.** Le Bourget is the first
position in nine of the twenty-three. That is an ordinary long-range business-jet routing with a
European fuel and customs stop, and it is what these traces mostly look like.

### The recovered data corrects the spreadsheet in the auditor's favour

Five of the twenty-three carry an `inaccurate` verdict in the spreadsheet's own audit column, and in
three of those the audit's objection was to the **route string**, not to the aircraft being there at
all. The recovered traces settle those three, and they settle them **against the sheet and for the
auditor**:

| Row | The sheet says | The audit said | What the recovered track shows |
|---|---|---|---|
| OWENS-021 (2024-02-14) | Omaha to Wilmington | *"flew from Lincoln to Wilmington, not Omaha"* | Starts **KLNK Lincoln, 1.04 km**. Ends **KILG Wilmington, 0.82 km**. |
| OWENS-025 (2024-04-19) | route string disputed | *"4/19 Paris–Goose Bay–SLC–Provo. The arrival itself is real; the sheet's route string is wrong."* | Starts **LFPB Paris-Le Bourget, 6.67 km**. Ends **KPVU Provo, 1.45 km**. |
| OWENS-041 (2025-09-10) | origin Paris / Lincoln | *"Sheet origin is wrong (Paris/Lincoln vs Provo)"* | Starts **KPVU Provo, 1.37 km**. Ends **KILG Wilmington, 1.75 km**. |

This matters as a method result. Those audit corrections were made against **paid** data — a
FlightRadar24 Business subscription and roughly 950 dollars of FlightAware logs. The free volunteer
archives reproduce them independently, without an account and without a payment. **Where the paid
audit and the free archive can be compared, they agree.**

### 10 September 2025, the one date that matters most

Two rows in the table fall on the day of the assassination, and both are worth reading closely
because the departure timing has been disputed three separate ways.

**SU-BTT** was heard on the Provo ramp from **13:07:53 UTC**, and both archives record the same
**wheels-up at 13:14:03.82 UTC** — agreeing with each other to the *millisecond*. Utah was on MDT
(UTC−6) that day, so that is **07:14:03 a.m. local**. The trace runs on to Wilmington, Delaware, last
heard at **16:50:37 UTC**. Charlie Kirk was shot at **12:23:30 p.m. MDT**. The aircraft was airborne
and eastbound **five hours and nine minutes before the shot**, and was already at Wilmington when it
happened. That is the version the [Provo page](/Planes/following/Provo_KPVU_2024-04-19_to_2025-09-13/overview)
has carried as "07:14 am", and the primary record now supports it.

**SU-BND never moved.** Its first and last positions on 10 September are the same parking spot at
Provo to five decimal places — 40.2298 N, 111.7277 W — across 328 position reports.

The [Candace Owens](/People/candace-owens) broadcast of 17 November 2025 reported a transponder
cycle on that airframe: **on 09:05:36, off 10:34:05, on 12:40:53, off 13:29:40**, with the time base
not stated. Those four numbers are reproducible from the free archives, and **both archives agree on
all four boundaries independently**:

| | Recovered (UTC) | Read as UTC−7 | Utah local, MDT (UTC−6) | As broadcast |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First contact | 16:05:35 | 09:05:35 | **10:05:35** | 09:05:36 |
| Track goes quiet | 17:34:09 | 10:34:09 | **11:34:09** | 10:34:05 |
| Track returns | 19:40:53 | 12:40:53 | **13:40:53** | 12:40:53 |
| Last contact | 20:29:38 | 13:29:38 | **14:29:38** | 13:29:40 |

The broadcast numbers match the recovered record **to within four seconds on every boundary** when
they are read as **UTC−7**. Utah on 10 September 2025 was on **UTC−6**. Applying the correct offset
moves every event **one hour later**: the transponder fell silent at **11:34 a.m. MDT — 49 minutes
before the shot** — and came back at **1:40 p.m. MDT**, which is **77 minutes after** the shot
rather than the 17 minutes the broadcast described.

**State plainly what that is and is not.** It is a timezone reconciliation, drawn from two
independent archives that agree to the second, and it is the same class of error the site has
already had to correct once on the SU-BTT departure clock. It is **not** an accusation that anyone
misled anybody — a one-hour offset on a UTC conversion is the commonest mistake in this entire
subject. And a gap in a volunteer receiver network's coverage of a **parked** aircraft is exactly
what a gap in a volunteer receiver network's coverage of a parked aircraft looks like. Ordinary
explanations — the APU cycling, ground power, a single receiver going offline — come first. The
aircraft's position is identical either side of the gap, so whatever happened, **it did not move**.

## The two that do not check out

Two of the twenty-three put the aircraft on the wrong continent.

### SU-BND, 12 May 2023 — alleged St. Louis, Missouri

The recovered track runs **Paris-Le Bourget (4.18 km) to Inshas Air Base, Egypt (19.8 km)** — 577
position reports, a full transatlantic-side leg from France into Egypt. On the date the spreadsheet
places this aircraft in Missouri, the primary record has it flying from France to Egypt.

### Country, or actually the city? Checked both ways

The automatic test in `analyse_overlap_recovery.js` is deliberately crude: it asks only whether the
recovered track puts the aircraft **in the alleged country**. A crude test is the right test to
automate, because it cannot be talked into a match.

But it under-states what came back, so the same 23 rows were also checked **against the alleged
metro area** — does the track actually reach the city the spreadsheet names, allowing for the
ordinary satellite fields (Valley Center for Wichita, Cahokia for St. Louis, Orem and Salt Lake for
Provo, New Castle for Wilmington)?

**The answer is the same twenty-one.** Every row that passes the country test also reaches the
alleged city. Not one of the 23 is a case of "the aircraft was somewhere in America that day, so
call it a match". The two failures are the two below, and they are the same two either way.

### SU-BTT, 18 June 2025 — alleged Omaha / Lincoln, Nebraska

The recovered track runs **Alexandria International, Egypt (36.13 km) to El Alamein International,
Egypt (75.92 km)**.

**This one is far thinner than the first, and the label "INCONSISTENT" overstates it.** Read the
timestamps, which is what settles it:

| | airplanes.live | adsb.lol |
|---|---|---|
| Points | **9** | **7** |
| First heard | **06:23:26 UTC** — 31.2496 N, 29.6159 E | 06:23:26 UTC — identical |
| Last heard | **06:28:54 UTC** — 31.4943 N, 28.9008 E | 06:28:54 UTC — identical |
| Span | **5 minutes 28 seconds** | the same 5 minutes |

**That is the whole record for the day: five and a half minutes, shortly after dawn, off the
Egyptian Mediterranean coast.** The two archives agree to the second, so the fragment is real — a
receiver heard SU-BTT there that morning. **What happens after 06:29 UTC is simply not recorded by
either archive.**

And that matters, because a Falcon 7X leaving Egypt at dawn can be in Nebraska the same calendar
day. Cairo to the American Midwest is roughly thirteen hours with the usual European tech stop;
06:29 UTC plus thirteen hours is **19:30 UTC, which is 14:30 in Lincoln**. **The fragment does not
rule out the alleged arrival. It only shows where the aircraft was at breakfast time.**

So the honest verdict on this row is **not "refuted" but "not supported, and not testable from what
survives"** — and it belongs in a different class from the SU-BND row above, which has a 577-point
track flying the wrong way for nearly four hours. The table marks both INCONSISTENT because the
script's test is a same-day country check; **this paragraph is the correction to that label**, and
it is here rather than buried because the difference between the two rows is large.

### What the spreadsheet's own audit already said about these two

**Both of them were already flagged.** Neither is a new finding:

| Row | `audit_verdict` | `survives_audit` | The counterargument column, already published |
|---|---|---|---|
| OWENS-050 | `inaccurate` | `no` | *"Kanekoa audit against FlightRadar24 Business and 950 dollars of FlightAware logs places the aircraft in Egypt / on another continent on this date. Counted in the 73 anyway because it is on the Owens sheet. Auditor: Cairo."* |
| OWENS-036 | `inaccurate` | `no` | *"Kanekoa audit against FlightRadar24 Business and 950 dollars of FlightAware logs places the aircraft in Egypt / on another continent on this date. Counted in the 73 anyway because it is on the Owens sheet."* |

So the honest reading is narrow and it cuts both ways.

**What it does mean:** the free archives independently reproduce a paid audit's conclusion on both
rows, from a completely different data source, at zero cost. Anyone can check it. That is a good
result for the method.

**What it does not mean:** two wrong rows do not condemn a 73-row sheet. The sheet's own audit column
had already marked both of them inaccurate and both as failing the audit, and they were counted in
the 73 anyway because they appear on the source spreadsheet — a fact the site has published all
along. **The error was already disclosed by the people compiling it.** Finding it again is
confirmation, not exposure.

## The thirty-nine with nothing in the window

Thirty-nine of the sixty-nine pairs returned nothing from either archive on the alleged day or
either side of it. **That is not a finding about deletion, and nobody should read it as one.**

The year-by-year breakdown, and it explains most of the shortfall on its own:

| Year | Pairs tested | Track on the alleged day | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| **2022** | 9 | **0** | **0%** — untestable by construction |
| 2023 | 23 | 8 | 35% |
| 2024 | 22 | 7 | 32% |
| 2025 | 15 | 8 | 53% |
| **Total** | **69** | **23** | **33%** |

The reasons, in order of how much they explain:

1. **Both free daily archives begin in 2023.** Every one of the **nine** 2022 rows was untestable
   before a single request was sent. A 404 on a 2022 date carries no information at all. The only
   free route into 2022 is the ADS-B Exchange monthly sample archive, which holds **one day per
   month** — see [the overview](/Planes/Flight-Data-Recovery/overview).
2. **These are volunteer receiver networks with real coverage holes.** A foreign-registered jet over
   the North Atlantic, over the Sahara, or on approach to Cairo is outside the dense ground-station
   coverage that makes a US or European track look complete. Coverage over Egypt in particular is
   sparse, and both of the archives here depend on somebody having voluntarily put an antenna on a
   roof.
3. **The two archives are not the same network.** `adsb.lol` and `globe.airplanes.live` are fed by
   different volunteers, which is why they were both asked. When neither has anything, two
   independent networks heard nothing — which is more informative than one silence, but is still
   silence.
4. **The aircraft may simply not have flown that day.** A parked aeroplane with no power on the bus
   produces no ADS-B. Long stationary periods are documented for both of these airframes — SU-BND
   sat at Provo for 113 continuous days in 2025.
5. **The claimed date may be wrong.** Seven of the misses turned out to be exactly that; they are the
   next section.

Concentrated by aircraft, the misses are **37 of SU-BTT's 53 pairs** and **9 of SU-BND's 16** — and
all nine of the 2022 rows are SU-BTT.

### The last free door: adsb.lol's own off-site backup, asked and closed

Points 2 and 3 above are an argument, not a measurement. So the measurement was taken.

`adsb.lol` mirrors its entire historical database to **public GitHub Releases** — one release per
day, roughly 2 GB, under the Open Database Licence. That is a different service from the live API,
and it holds days the live API refuses. Every alleged-overlap date **from 2024 onward** that both
daily archives missed — **22 dates** — was put to it. Each release was streamed straight through
`tar` and filtered, so about 44 GB crossed the wire and nothing but a matching trace could have
touched the disk.

| | |
|---|---|
| Dates asked | **22** |
| Traces recovered | **0** |
| Verdict on every one | `NOT_IN_THE_BACKUP_EITHER` |

**A sweep that returns nothing 22 times out of 22 is exactly what a broken script also returns**, so
before any of it was believed the same code was pointed at a **control**: SU-BTT on **1 April 2023**,
a date the adsb.lol live API is known to serve. It came back `RECOVERED_FROM_OFFSITE_BACKUP` with the
trace in hand. The extraction works. The misses are real.

**What that establishes, stated narrowly.** The backup is *the same organisation's data* as the live
API, so a miss here is **not** independent corroboration — it cannot tell you anything the live 404
did not. What it does do is close the last free door: for those 22 dates, "no adsb.lol receiver
recorded this aircraft" is now a **checked** statement rather than an inference from an API response.
It remains **no evidence whatever that anything was removed**.

**Three bugs were found and fixed on our side before these numbers were trusted**, and they are
recorded because each one would have produced a *false statement about the archive*:

* Most daily releases ship the tar **split** as `.tar.aa` + `.tar.ab`. An early run asked for a
  single `.tar`, got 404, and recorded **16 published dates as "not available"**.
* A transient API or download failure was being written down as a verdict. Two dates were wrongly
  marked unavailable; both are published, and both re-ran to genuine misses.
* A few days ship **both** a small standalone `.tar` and a large split pair — `v2024.03.22` is the
  one in this set. Concatenating all three produces a stream `tar` cannot read, and that failure
  looks identical to "the aircraft is not in there". **All 22 dates were audited for this layout
  before any verdict was accepted.**

The script now reports `COULD_NOT_ASK` and `DOWNLOAD_FAILED_RETRY_THIS` as distinct from a real miss,
so a failure on our end can never again be recorded as a fact about somebody else's archive. Raw
record: `data/recovery/github_backup_sweep.json`. Re-run it with
`python3 sweep_github_backup.py`.

## The seven that landed on the day next door

Seven pairs had no track on the alleged day but **did** have one on an adjacent day:

| Overlap ID | Aircraft | Alleged date | Track actually found on | Offset |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SITE-001 | SU-BTT | 2023-04-02 | 2023-04-01 | day before |
| OWENS-016 | SU-BND | 2023-06-12 | 2023-06-11 | day before |
| OWENS-052 | SU-BND | 2023-06-25 | 2023-06-24 | day before |
| OWENS-055 | SU-BTT | 2024-04-15 | 2024-04-16 | day after |
| OWENS-028 | SU-BTT | 2024-07-20 | 2024-07-21 | day after |
| OWENS-029 | SU-BTT | 2024-08-17 | 2024-08-18 | day after |
| OWENS-030 | SU-BND | 2024-12-08 | 2024-12-07 | day before |

**A one-day offset is often not a different flight.** These archives are indexed by **UTC date**. An
aircraft leaving an American field at 7 p.m. Central is airborne at 01:00 UTC the following day, so
the same physical flight is filed under tomorrow. A transatlantic leg routinely spans two UTC dates
outright.

**But do not over-claim it.** A one-day offset is also exactly what a genuinely wrong date in a
spreadsheet looks like, and nothing in a date index can tell those two apart. These seven are
**neither confirmed nor refuted**. They are counted separately from the 23 for that reason, and they
are not added to the "consistent" tally. Each would need its own timestamp-by-timestamp check
against the alleged event before it could be called anything.

## What this changes, and what it does not

### It changes this

* **Rows this site published as untestable now have primary data behind them.** Twenty-three of the
  sixty-nine have a real ADS-B trace, from two independent archives, saved to disk in this repository
  with the source in the filename.
* **Twenty-one of the twenty-three corroborate the flight leg**, and fourteen of the recovered tracks
  end within two kilometres of an airport. The legs are largely real.
* **On three rows the recovered data corrects the spreadsheet toward the auditor**, including the
  10 September origin. The free archives reproduced a paid audit's corrections at no cost.
* **The 10 September departure clock is settled by primary data**: SU-BTT first airborne fix 13:14:03.82 UTC (last on-ground fix 13:13:16 UTC, so rotation is inside a 47-second window),
  07:14 a.m. MDT, five hours before the shot, with both archives agreeing to the millisecond.
* **Two rows are contradicted outright** by the primary record, both of them already flagged
  `inaccurate` by the sheet's own audit.

### It does not change this

* **The overlap pairing is still unsourced.** Every line above is about an aircraft. Whether a Kirk
  was in that city on that date is a separate question, and the recovered data says nothing about it.
* **Erika Kirk's itinerary is still absent, and no archive fixes it.** There is no dated, published
  list of her locations for a backup to restore. The 73-overlap tally is measured against something
  this repository cannot reproduce.
* **The audit's central finding stands.** The individual flight legs verify at a high rate while the
  *overlaps* built on top of them do not. That was true before this sweep and it is still true after
  it — this page is a large body of evidence for the first half of that sentence and none at all for
  the second.
* **Thirty-nine rows remain untested**, nine of them permanently so at free-tier cost.
* **Presence is never purpose.** Every aircraft here has ordinary explanations available —
  maintenance at the Duncan Aviation facilities these airframes repeatedly visit, diplomatic travel,
  routine positioning — and those explanations are documented elsewhere on this site alongside the
  claims.
* **None of this is in the criminal case.** No flight record features in the prosecution of
  [Tyler Robinson](/Tyler_Robinson/overview), who is charged and has not been convicted.

## Where this page fits

Every row of [the 73-overlap spreadsheet](/Planes/following/73_overlaps) that reduces to an aircraft and a date was pulled against two free daily ADS-B archives, which is the first time [the following claim](/Planes/Following-Charlie-Erika) has been asked of primary position data instead of screenshots — two tails, [SU-BTT](/Planes/SU-BTT/overview) and [SU-BND](/Planes/SU-BND/overview), carry the whole set, and [Provo](/Planes/following/Provo_KPVU_2024-04-19_to_2025-09-13/overview) is where they cluster.
