# Per-Aircraft Recovery Status

This is the reference page. Pick one tail number and get a complete answer: which archives still hold
its ADS-B tracks, how far back each one reaches, how many copies of its tracking-site page the
Internet Archive kept, and how many rows of published flight history were pulled back out of those
copies.

**The headline first, because it is the same for all sixteen: no page was found removed.** Sixteen
aircraft were probed on five tracking sites — flightradar24.com, flightaware.com, radarbox.com,
globe.adsbexchange.com and planespotters.net — and every aircraft page that could be tested loads
today. The HTTP 403s this site once read as removals are robot blocks that hit unrelated control
aircraft identically. That correction, with the control test behind it, is on
[Flight Data Recovery](/Planes/Flight-Data-Recovery/overview) and
[What A 403 Actually Means](/Planes/Flight-Data-Recovery/What-A-403-Means).

Everything below is generated from `fleet_recovery_matrix.json` and the `.meta.json` sidecar written
beside every payload under `site/docs/Planes/<TAIL>/data/recovered/`. The source is in every filename.

**How to read the columns.** The first three are days of full ADS-B trace recovered, one file per
aircraft per day. "Earliest date any source reaches" is the oldest date on which any free archive
holds a track for that airframe — for eleven of the sixteen that is now in 2022, a year this site
previously published as untestable. "Wayback page captures held" counts archived copies of the
tracking-site page itself, which is the only thing that could ever document a removal. The last
column counts rows of published flight history recovered out of those copies.

**A zero in the first three columns means a volunteer network heard nothing on those days.** It does
not mean the aircraft did not fly and it does not mean a transponder was off. Both free daily
archives begin in 2023, so every 2022 gap is a limit of the archive by construction.

## The four things worth stopping on

* **[N55906](/Planes/N55906/overview) has zero data in every archive** — zero adsb.lol, zero
  airplanes.live, zero ADS-B Exchange monthly samples, and zero flight rows on FlightRadar24 in a
  real browser. It is a Piper Cherokee Arrow II, a 1970s single-engine light aircraft. The honest
  reading is that it is not fitted with, or is not being received on, ADS-B. Detail below.
* **[N888KG](/Planes/N888KG/overview) is by far the best-archived page on the fleet** — 22 Wayback
  captures across fifteen years and **70 recovered flight rows**, nearly half the fleet total.
* **[T7-ELL](/Planes/T7ELL-EJM36/overview) reaches back to 1 February 2022** on the ADS-B Exchange
  monthly samples, the joint-oldest recovery on the following side.
* **[SU-BTU](/Planes/SU-BTU/overview) has zero adsb.lol days but three on airplanes.live** and nine
  monthly samples. Two volunteer networks with different receiver coverage produce different
  results for the same airframe. That is ordinary, and it is **not** evidence of anything.

### N55906

ICAO hex `A72351` · Piper Cherokee Arrow II (P28R) · United States registry · **n1098l** side ·
named in a John Cullen claim.

* **adsb.lol** — **0 days.**
* **airplanes.live** — **0 days.**
* **ADSBX monthly samples** — **0 days**, across all 56 months tried from January 2022 to August 2026.
* **Wayback** — 1 FlightAware capture, **4 Mar 2010**, saved but with no flight rows parsed. 0 rows
  recovered.
* **FlightRadar24, real browser, 24 Aug 2026** — HTTP 200, page loads with its identity block
  (`A72351` · P28R · Private owner), and **zero flight rows**, printing *"could not find data for
  specified flight"*.

**This is the only aircraft on the fleet with nothing in any archive, and the explanation is the
aircraft itself.** A Piper Cherokee Arrow II is a four-seat, single-engine, retractable-gear light
aircraft of 1970s design. Aircraft in that class are frequently not equipped with ADS-B Out that the
volunteer networks can hear, or are heard only when close to a receiver, and many are flown a few
hours a month. Four independent archives finding nothing, over four and a half years, is what that
looks like.

**What that does and does not establish.** It establishes that no free ADS-B source can put this
tail anywhere, on any date, and that its page has not been removed from anywhere — it loads
normally. It does not establish that the aircraft never flew. What it does bear on is the claim
itself: the tail was named as part of a survey-aircraft line of inquiry, and a 1970s light single is
not a survey platform in the sense that a Global Express or a Navajo is. `fleet.js` carries that
same note against the entry. The point here is narrow and it is about the data, not about the person
who made the claim.

## How to check any line on this page

Nothing on this page needs an account, an API key or a payment. Run these from
`site/docs/Planes/following/apis/public_open_source/code/` and compare the output to the numbers
above.

```
# both free daily archives, day by day, for one tail
node recover_erased.js --tail N102DZ

# the free ADS-B Exchange monthly sample archive, back to 2016
node recover_adsbx_samples.js --tail SU-BTT

# every alleged overlap date for one tail, pulled on the day and the day either side
node recover_overlaps.js --tail SU-BTT
```

Each run writes its payloads to `site/docs/Planes/<TAIL>/data/recovered/` with **the source in the
filename**, and a `.meta.json` beside every file recording the exact URL, the HTTP status, the byte
count and the UTC retrieval time. **Nothing overwrites a previous pull** — a re-pull lands beside the
first with its own timestamp, because the difference between two pulls is how a disappearance would
be shown.

Two counts on this page do not come from those three commands. The **Wayback page captures** and the
**flight rows off archived pages** columns come from the Internet Archive sweep recorded in
`fleet_recovery_matrix.json`, and the browser row counts quoted in the per-aircraft sections come
from `browser_capture/captures/fr24_page_availability_2026-08-24.tsv`.
