# Getting The Data

The claim that foreign-registered aircraft shadowed Charlie Kirk, Erika Kirk and TPUSA rests almost
entirely on **third-hand readings of flight-tracking websites**. Somebody photographed an
aircraft-history table. Somebody typed the rows into a spreadsheet. Somebody tallied the spreadsheet.
Somebody read the tally on television. **Every one of those steps is a place a row could be misread**
— and the published audits say a great many of them were.

This section replaces that chain with data we pull ourselves, from named sources, with the raw
response saved and timestamped. It exists so that a reader who does not believe a word of this site
can repeat the pulls and get the same answer.

**[3 — Government records](/Planes/following/apis/government/knowledge)**

**[2 — Commercial APIs](/Planes/following/apis/proprietary/knowledge)**
The deep history the free networks do not hold. Costs money; terms restrict republication.
**Not run.** No credential held for any vendor — reported by name, never by value.

**[4 — Website capture](/Planes/following/apis/browser_capture/knowledge)**
Driving a real browser at the tracking sites. How we document that data is gone.
**Started.** One site of eight, 20 targets of 128 — and it refuted one of our own claims.

A pass is never a substitute for another. **The same claim checked four ways and agreeing is the
strongest thing this investigation can produce. Checked four ways and disagreeing is the second
strongest — and it gets published too.**

## What the first pass found

478 pulls against the free historical archive on 24 August 2026. 178 traces retrieved. Three results
worth stating up front.

**The September 10 departure has a primary answer.** SU-BTT was on the ground at Provo Municipal at
13:07 UTC — 07:07 Mountain — lifted off at 13:14 UTC, and was on the ground at Wilmington New Castle,
Delaware at 16:50 UTC. That is a departure about five hours before the assassination and a landing
about ninety minutes before it, from a 1,229-point track. It matches the "7:08 AM MT" figure that has
circulated and is inconsistent with the competing accounts. **It says where an airframe was. It says
nothing about who was aboard or who tasked it, and no public record establishes tasking in either
direction.**

**An independent check agrees with the audit.** Running every exactly-claimed date through primary
traces, blind: pairings this repo's audit called **accurate** have a trace **75%** of the time, ones
called **partial 88%**, and ones called **inaccurate only 11%**.

**And the sharpest result splits the claim in two.** Individual flight legs — "this aircraft was at
this field on this date" — verify at **98%**. Overlap pairings — "and therefore it was following
them" — verify at **38%**. **The aircraft movements are largely real. The inference that they
constitute shadowing is where the spreadsheet falls apart.** That finding flatters neither side of
this argument, which is roughly the point.

## Where the four passes stand now — 24 August 2026

The figures above are from the first sweep. A later review re-ran the work with **control aircraft**
in every basket, and the controls changed several conclusions — including three of our own.

| | |
|---|---|
| Historical archives now in use | **3** — adsb.lol, globe.airplanes.live, and the ADS-B Exchange monthly sample |
| Aircraft-days recovered from the **backup** archive that adsb.lol does not hold | **152** |
| Aircraft-days held by adsb.lol that the backup does not hold | **0** |
| Aircraft-days held by **both** archives across **1–16 September 2025** | **74**, against 1 backup-only |
| Aircraft-days now held from **before 2023** | **34**, across 12 tails |
| Claimed overlaps tested against primary position data | **85 of 85** |
| Of those, **decidable** from primary data | **29** — **25 corroborated, 1 same-metro, 3 refuted** |
| Refutations published on 24 Aug and **retracted** on 26 Aug after a fix-by-fix re-read | **9 of 12** |
| Flight legs re-extracted from archived pages that had been recorded as zero | **153** |
| Internet Archive CDX queries, re-run without `collapse=digest` | **30 of 30 succeeded, 0 failed** |
| 200 → 404 transitions found anywhere in the archive data | **None** |

**Three corrections to this site came out of that review, and they are published as corrections:**

* **The Flightradar24 403 is bot protection, site-wide.** It returns 403 to a script for 13 case
  tails, 7 unrelated control aircraft **and its own home page**, with the same ~5,877-byte body every
  time. **We had read a 403 as evidence a record was removed. It cannot mean that.** A browser session
  then loaded 20 of those pages — 16 subject tails, 4 controls — **HTTP 200 on every one**, and a
  second independent session reproduced it. **The claim that N102DZ's page was removed is refuted.**
  What the page displayed on a past date remains beyond every method we have, and we do not claim it.
* **The adsb.lol archive has two dead bands and both are site-wide** — 403 from exactly 12 October to
  about 30 December 2025, then 404 to about 1 August 2026, **identical for control aircraft with no
  connection to this case.** We had published the band as ending in December. **Neither band is
  scrubbing.**
* **Three "never archived" results were failed queries logged as zeros**, one of them SU-BTT's ADS-B
  Exchange page, which has three snapshots. The re-run reports a failed query as UNKNOWN instead of
  as zero.

**And the single most important limit, restated because the numbers above invite the opposite
reading.** Every one of the 25 corroborated overlaps is a statement about **an airframe**. **An ADS-B
record can locate an aircraft and can never locate a passenger.** None of it puts Charlie Kirk or
Erika Kirk anywhere. **A confirmed aircraft is not a confirmed overlap.**

## Where the passes stand — 28 August 2026

The four-pass design assumed the passes would each contribute. In practice one of
them has done all the work, and saying so plainly is more useful than pretending
otherwise.

| Pass | Aircraft-days produced | Status |
|---|---:|---|
| **1 — free and open** | **2,346** | **100% of every trace this investigation holds** |
| 2 — commercial | **0** | **No credential is held for any vendor.** Nothing was queried |
| 3 — government records | 0 traces | The FAA registry was pulled; **no records request has been filed anywhere** |
| 4 — browser capture | 0 traces | Ran, and its result stands: no aircraft page in this investigation has been removed |

**Pass 1's split, by source:** globe.airplanes.live 2,072 aircraft-days (371 of
them existing nowhere else), adsb.lol 1,702, ADS-B Exchange free monthly samples
207 (153 unique, and the only free route that reaches 2022), adsb.lol's GitHub
Release backup 113. Plus 66 archived web-page captures, which are counted in pages
and not in aircraft-days because a crawler's visit date is not a flight date.

**Pass 2 producing zero is a finding, not an embarrassment.** The credential slots
exist in the house credential store; an empty string means "not held", and the
client reports `BLOCKED on credential` by name without ever printing a value.
**FlightAware's AeroAPI reaches back to 2011 and is the highest-value unopened
door in the whole investigation.** It is also the only remaining way to raise the
per-tail coverage figure, because every free archive has now been asked every
event-window day for every aircraft in the fleet.

**Pass 3 is the genuinely under-used route**, and it is the one that produces
*records* rather than *readings*. Most of the fields in this case are city-owned
or run by a public airport authority, which makes **state public-records law** —
not federal FOIA — the route: landing fees, fuel tickets, ramp assignment, hangar
and badge-access logs. It is faster and cheaper than FOIA and nobody has filed
one.

Coverage percentages, the archive holes, and the defects found in this section's
own code are all accounted for on
[Investigating Deleted Flights](/Planes/investigating_deleted_flights).

## What none of it can do

**The counts remain trackers' tallies, not records.** 73, 72, 70+, 68, 29, 23 — amateur readings of
public data by independent researchers, never reconciled with each other. Nothing in this section
turns one of them into a verified total, and where they conflict this site shows the conflict rather
than averaging it.

**ADS-B has holes, and they cut both ways.** A missing trace means a volunteer network heard nothing.
It does not establish that an aircraft did not fly and it does not establish that a transponder was
switched off — a parked aircraft, a coverage gap over the Atlantic, and a wrong claimed date all look
identical. Every gap we hit is written down in a `MISSING_DATA.md` file beside the relevant aircraft
and overlap, **90 of them**, saying exactly that.

**One of the two shut doors has since been forced.** A later pass — run on the assumption that the
data had been erased, and looking for backups — found that **2022 is partly reachable after all**,
that days the primary archive refuses are published in full elsewhere, and that FlightRadar24 flight
history for [N102DZ](/Planes/N102DZ/overview) survives in the Internet Archive. That work is
**[Flight Data Recovery](/Planes/Flight-Data-Recovery/overview)**, and it corrects several statements
made in this section.

**One word in that paragraph was wrong and is corrected above:** the N102DZ history was described as
*removed*. **It was not removed.** The live Flightradar24 page loads normally in a browser; the empty
table is the free tier's seven-day window, and an unrelated control aircraft prints the identical
"could not find data" message at almost the identical byte count. The archived copy is still worth
holding — the seven-day window means the live page cannot show those legs to a logged-out visitor —
but **holding a copy is not the same as documenting a removal**, and this site said the second when it
had only the first.

**The other door is still shut, and no backup opens it.** **Erika Kirk's side cannot be checked at
all.** Every recovery in that pass is about *aircraft*, and an aircraft track never establishes who
was aboard. This repo holds a single sourced location for her before 10 September 2025, against a
window somebody counted 73 overlaps across. See
[Erika Kirk's flight logs reported erased](/Planes/Erika-Flight-Logs-Erased).

## What this section will not do

It will not merge the threads. The 18-month following pattern, the September 10 day-of timeline, the
N1098L thread and the N888KG Wendover departure are **four different claims about four different sets
of aircraft**, and blending them is how the whole angle gets dismissed at once.

It will not name who tasked an aircraft, because no public record establishes it. It will not name a
living person as an accused. And it will not publish a page without the innocent explanation beside
the suspicious one — including the innocent explanation for our own results, which is that an
aircraft simply was not there.

## Where this page fits

The following-planes claim rests on third-hand readings of tracking websites, and this page is the plan for replacing that chain with data pulled directly — four passes covering [free open sources](/Planes/following/apis/public_open_source/knowledge), [commercial APIs](/Planes/following/apis/proprietary/knowledge), [government records](/Planes/following/apis/government/knowledge) and [browser captures of the sites themselves](/Planes/following/apis/browser_capture/knowledge), each raw response saved and timestamped for every page in the [Following log](/Planes/following/overview). The point is that a reader who believes none of this site can repeat the pulls and get the same answer, which is a standard the rest of the case never gets held to — [the sealed warrants](/Legal/Evidence-Sealing-2026), [the halted foreign-nexus inquiry](/US_Intelligence/nctc) and [the withheld medical record](/Medical/overview) are all places where nobody outside can check anything. Read [what a 403 actually means](/Planes/Flight-Data-Recovery/What-A-403-Means) next for the run where these passes corrected this site rather than confirming it: an HTTP 403 published here as a documented removal turned out to be a blanket anti-bot response, and five unrelated control aircraft failed exactly the same way.
