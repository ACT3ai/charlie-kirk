# Commercial Flight-Data APIs

**We have not bought any of this yet. Every question below is open, and the page exists so that
stays visible instead of quietly becoming "no comment".**

The free sources answered a great deal — see [the open-source
pass](/Planes/following/apis/public_open_source/knowledge) — but they stop dead in two places that
matter, and commercial vendors are the only way through both.

**The counterargument to every row of that table, stated at full strength:** each of these is a statement about how a website responds to a client on one day. **None of them is a statement about whether an aircraft flew.**

**A missing credential is reported by NAME, never by value.** The slots exist in the house credential store outside every git repo; an empty string means "not held" and the client exits with `BLOCKED on credential` and the key's name. **No script on this site ever prints a credential value.**

## Read this before you read anything else on this page

**Flightradar24 returns HTTP 403 to scripted requests across its entire site, including its own home
page. A 403 from Flightradar24 says nothing whatsoever about any particular aircraft's record — and
this site has been reading it as though it did.**

That is our error and it is stated first because everything else on this page depends on it.

### The control test, run 24 August 2026

| Probed | HTTP |
|---|---|
| `flightradar24.com/data/aircraft/n102dz` | **403** |
| The other 12 case tails — SU-BTT, SU-BND, SU-BTU, SU-BTV, SU-BGM, N888KG, N40JD, N560TW, N582MM, N872RA, N1098L, T7-ELL | **403 — every one** |
| **7 unrelated control aircraft** with no connection to this case — N628TS, G-EUUU, VH-OQA, N509AY, D-AIBA, N1, EI-DYY | **403 — every one** |
| `https://www.flightradar24.com/` — the **home page** | **403** |
| `https://www.flightradar24.com/data/aircraft` — the index | **403** |

**Every single response is the same body, roughly 5,877 bytes.** A block that treats an airliner in
everyday service, a retired museum aircraft and the front door of the site itself identically is
**bot protection**. It is not a decision about an aeroplane, and it **cannot distinguish a removed
record from a present one.**

### What that corrects on this site

`Planes/Flight-Data-Recovery/overview` recorded N102DZ's Flightradar24 page as **"A REAL REMOVAL,
DOCUMENTED"**, reasoning that the Internet Archive holds a populated copy while the live URL returns
403 to the public today. **The second half of that reasoning does not survive the control test.**

And then the browser pass went and looked. **In a real logged-out browser session the page loads
normally — HTTP 200, full identity block, title "N102DZ - Gulfstream V [555] - Flightradar24", MODE S
`A00C85`, type code `GLF5`. Nothing on it says removed, blocked, restricted or unavailable.** Twenty
aircraft pages were probed that way — 16 subject tails and 4 unrelated controls — and **every single
one returned 200.** A second, independent session re-ran six of them and agreed.

**The claim "N102DZ was removed from Flightradar24" is REFUTED.** Not untested — refuted. We reported
it more strongly than the evidence supported, and a control test plus a browser session is what
corrected it. The captures are at
`apis/browser_capture/captures/fr24_page_availability_2026-08-24.tsv` and
`fr24_n102dz_removal_test_2026-08-24b.tsv`.

**The limit travels with the refutation everywhere it is published.** A page that loads today cannot
testify about what its table held in May 2026. **"The page was taken down" is refuted. A narrower
claim — that history was specifically purged and later restored — is beyond this method in either
direction, and no page on this site may assert it.** A logged-out free tier is also the weakest view
Flightradar24 offers.

### And the corollary that has to be understood with it

**Flightradar24's free tier shows seven days of aircraft history.** That single fact dissolved what
looked like the strongest removal in this whole investigation.

The Internet Archive holds 14 Flightradar24 captures of the **N888KG** page. The flights-history table
is **populated** on 11 September 2025 (three captures), 12 September and 13 September — and **empty**
on every capture from 18 September 2025 through 28 January 2026. Same URL, same site, **all HTTP
200**. The byte count drops from 63,886 to 56,647.

That is exactly the shape of a record being pulled. It is not one. The empty captures carry
Flightradar24's own text:

> "Sorry, but we could not find data for specified flight. More than 7 days of N888KG history is
> available with an upgrade to a Silver (90 days), Gold (1 year), or Business (3 years)
> subscription."

**The 10 September 2025 flight was visible on 11–13 September because it was inside seven days, and
had rolled out of the window by 18 September.** Every Flightradar24 capture we hold is consistent with
that window and with nothing else.

**The browser session then supplied the control that proves it.** N102DZ prints the "could not find
data for specified flight" message at **53,310 bytes**; the **unrelated control N628TS prints the
identical message at 53,318 bytes.** N888KG prints it too, at 53,338. Meanwhile SU-BTT, SU-BND and a
Ryanair control all render full history in the same session, because they had flown recently. **The
empty table tracks whether the aircraft flew in the last seven days, and nothing else.** Every row in
that capture, subject and control alike, carries the upgrade paywall notice.

**Corrected 15 September 2026 — the empty table was not the seven-day window.** The page was not taken down, and that still stands. But volunteer ADS-B receivers heard N102DZ fly on 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22 and 24 August 2026, all inside the seven days before the 24 August browser test, so its empty table cannot have been the free window. The control N628TS was not heard flying by free receivers on any of those days, so its empty table was genuine idleness while N102DZ's was not. We retested on 15 September 2026 with a control that had flown: in the same browser session Flightradar24 listed [N560TW](/Planes/N560TW/overview)'s two flights of 9 September, and showed "could not find data" for N102DZ, which receivers heard fly on 12, 13 and 14 September. **Flightradar24 hides N102DZ's flights while still showing the aircraft. That is hiding, not deletion, and no archived copy of the page dates when it began.** FlightAware hides them too: its own page data marks N102DZ blocked, and already did on 14 January 2026. **From now on a control only counts if free receivers show it flew inside the window.** Full retest: [Which Flight Records Were Deleted](/Planes/Deleted-Flight-Records#updated-15-september-2026--two-tracking-sites-are-hiding-four-of-these-aircrafts-flights).

**Corrected 15 September 2026 — not every N888KG capture fits the window.** The captures of 18–20 September 2025 and 28 January 2026 do: free receivers hold no N888KG flight in the seven days before any of them. The **2 November 2025** capture does not. It is empty, yet the ADS-B Exchange sample has N888KG taking off from and landing back at Provo on **1 November 2025**. FlightAware's own page data had already marked N888KG **blocked** on 11 September 2025. So the September drop is the window closing, and by November Flightradar24 was hiding this aircraft's flights. See [Which Flight Records Were Deleted](/Planes/Deleted-Flight-Records#updated-15-september-2026--two-tracking-sites-are-hiding-four-of-these-aircrafts-flights).

**Both facts have to be held at once before any Flightradar24 observation is worth anything:** a 403
is a robot block, and an empty free-tier table is a retention window. Neither is evidence about an
aircraft. **This page publishes its own dissolved claim because a site that only reports the findings
that survive is not reporting.**

### FlightAware, by contrast, answers

The same scripted requests to FlightAware return **HTTP 200.** It is not blocking us, and it remains a
usable route.

Its modern aircraft pages are a client-side application, so an archived copy of one holds **no
server-rendered flight rows** — the Archive saved the frame, not the data. Its **pre-2017** pages are
server-rendered tables, and **109 flight legs** were recovered out of them. Details are in
[the open-source pass](/Planes/following/apis/public_open_source/knowledge).

## What the free pass cannot reach

1. **2022.** The free historical archive we found does not go back that far. The earliest trace we
   obtained anywhere is 24 February 2023. The trackers date the whole pattern from **2022**. Those
   rows are currently untestable at zero cost.
   **— NARROWED.** ADS-B Exchange's free monthly sample archive reaches 2016 and has produced 34
   aircraft-days before 2023. But it is **one day per month**, so a claim about a specific mid-month
   2022 date is still untestable for free. The commercial case below stands; it is just smaller.
2. **The rows with no trace.** 52 exactly-claimed dates returned nothing from the volunteer network.
   A commercial network with denser receiver coverage and satellite ADS-B may hold some of them —
   and a vendor with deep coverage *also* finding nothing is a considerably stronger negative than a
   volunteer network finding nothing.

## What APIs they have

### Flightradar24 — the official API

The vendor whose screenshots this entire claim was read off, which makes its own API the most direct
way to check whether those screenshots were read correctly.

The endpoint that matters is **flight summary** — historic and near-real-time synopses of flights,
queryable by registration and date range. It is the machine-readable form of the aircraft-history
table people photographed. There are also historic flight-positions and full flight-track endpoints.

Bearer token, credit-metered, subscription.

**And it is the only way to see Flightradar24 at all from a script.** The public web pages return 403
to everything automated, as shown above. Without the API or a real browser, this vendor is opaque to
us — which is precisely why a 403 from it has to be reported as opacity rather than as a finding.

### FlightAware AeroAPI v4

**History back to 1 January 2011** — deep enough to cover the entire 2022 to 2025 window the
trackers counted across. That made it the single most valuable unopened door on this page, and it was opened on 14 September 2026 ([results](#update--15-september-2026-the-aeroapi-key-is-held-and-it-has-been-used)).

`GET /history/flights/{registration}?ident_type=registration` with a start and end is the call,
and **one call covers at most seven days**. Header is `x-apikey`. Run
`GET /aircraft/{registration}/blocked` first: a tail in an FAA privacy programme returns no
history, and that is a lawful filing, not a removal. Results are cursor-paginated and **every page
is a separately billed result set of up to 15 records**; FlightAware lists history lookups at
$0.020 per result set.

**CORRECTED 14 September 2026.** This section previously gave `GET /aircraft/{registration}/flights`
as the call and "around $0.002 per query" as the price. That path is not an AeroAPI v4 resource,
and the published history price is ten times that figure.

### ADS-B Exchange, via RapidAPI

Once the unfiltered community feed — and this is the part that bears on the investigation. Several of
the original claims were read off `globe.adsbexchange.com` history pages while they were open. **Those
pages now return HTTP 403 to the public** (checked 24 August 2026). The data did not vanish; it moved
behind a subscription. Anyone re-checking an old screenshot against that site today will find they
cannot, and may reasonably but wrongly conclude something was removed.

### Others worth knowing

RadarBox (AirNav) runs an independent receiver network, which makes it a genuine cross-check rather
than another view of the same feed. Cirium and OAG are scheduled-airline oriented and largely
irrelevant to business jets. Spire sells satellite ADS-B, which is the only thing that would cover the
mid-Atlantic legs nobody's ground receivers can see.

## How you use them

Clients are in `apis/proprietary/code/` — `fr24api.js`, `aeroapi.js`,
`adsbexchange_rapidapi.js`. **None of them run without a credential, and that is deliberate.** Each
one exits with the reason, and the reason is meant to be copied into this page as a
blocked-on-credential entry. Since 14 September 2026 `aeroapi.js` also has a **dry run, and it is
the default**: without `--run` it prints the exact call and bills nothing, so anyone can see what
would be asked before paying for it.

```
node fr24api.js SU-BTT 2025-09-01 2025-09-15
node aeroapi.js SU-BTT 2025-09-04 2025-09-11          # dry run - prints the call, bills nothing
node aeroapi.js SU-BTT 2025-09-04 2025-09-11 --run    # billed; at most 7 days per call
node aeroapi.js --probe N102DZ --run                  # blocked, last_flight, owner
node adsbexchange_rapidapi.js SU-BTT
```

**Credentials never live in this repo.** They are read at run time by
`public_open_source/code/lib/credentials.js`, which looks in two places, in this order:

1. the environment — `export FR24_API_TOKEN=...` still works and still wins;
2. a credential file outside every git repo, `~/.credentials/charlie_kirk.json`, mode `600`, one
   key per vendor under `charlie_kirk.flight_apis`.

Never a file in this repo, never a commit, never a page, never printed. A reader repeating our
pulls supplies their own credential the same way — that is the point of publishing the method
rather than the key.

## What their terms allow us to publish

**This is the constraint that shapes every page built from this pass.**

Commercial terms generally let you *use* the data and *state a conclusion*. They do not let you
mirror the feed. So the rule here is: **publish the finding, not the payload.** The raw response
stays in `apis/proprietary/data/` as our own audit trail, and the page says the response is held but
not published, names the vendor, and gives the date of the query.

Where a licence is unclear, we treat it as restrictive.

Compare that with the [government pass](/Planes/following/apis/government/knowledge), where the data
is public domain and mirroring it in full is the right thing to do. **The pass a fact came from
determines what may be done with it.**

## The rule that governs this pass

**Run the free pass first, always.** adsb.lol serves a free historical archive at the same URL shape
ADS-B Exchange charges for. Never buy an answer that is already free, and never spend on a sweep when
a named question would do — "did SU-BTT land at Wilmington on 10 September 2025, and when" is a
question; "get everything about SU-BTT" is a bill.

And never work around a paywall. No scraping a vendor's site, no undocumented endpoints, no shared
keys. **A blocked question recorded honestly is worth more to this investigation than an answer
obtained badly** — because the answer obtained badly is the first thing a hostile reader will use to
throw out everything else.

## Where this page fits

Commercial vendors hold the deep history the free networks do not — including the whole 2022 window at the start of the claimed period — so this page inside [the data section](/Planes/following/apis/overview) is deliberately a list of unbought, open questions rather than a page of results, sitting beside [what the free sources did return](/Planes/following/apis/public_open_source/knowledge) and [what the archives got back](/Planes/Flight-Data-Recovery/overview). It opens with this site's own error, because a Flightradar24 403 was published here as a documented removal and it was not one, and that kind of retraction is what [the evidence-threshold guide](/Fix/Conclusion_Logic), [the counterargument pages](/Theories/overview) and [the open-questions timeline](/Timeline/open-questions-gaps-timeline) exist to force. Read [what a 403 actually means](/Planes/Flight-Data-Recovery/What-A-403-Means) next for the control test in full: five unrelated aircraft with no connection to this case returned exactly the same status on exactly the same endpoint, which is the difference between a paywall and a deletion — and the same test now applies to [the N102DZ page](/Planes/N102DZ/overview) that [Erika Kirk invited people to check](/Planes/Erika-Flight-Logs-Erased).
