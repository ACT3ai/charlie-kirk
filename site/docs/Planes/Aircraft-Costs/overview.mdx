# Aircraft Costs — What Every Plane in This Case Costs New

Every aircraft in this investigation, priced as if you walked into a factory today and bought one. **33 airframes. Roughly $1.10 billion** at current list prices.

That number is the point of this page. The following-planes claim is often argued as though a jet showing up somewhere is a light thing. It is not. **A Falcon 7X parked at a regional Utah airport for six days is a $58 million asset sitting idle**, with a crew on per-diem, in a place with no scheduled reason to be. Whatever the explanation turns out to be — maintenance, diplomacy, or something else — it is an expensive explanation. Cost is context, and this page supplies it.

## How these figures were built

Most of the aircraft in this case are **no longer in production**. A 1974 Navajo Chieftain and a 1998 Gulfstream V cannot be bought new at any price. So each figure is derived one of two ways, and the method is named in every row:

* **Still in production** → the current **manufacturer list price** for that exact type.
* **Out of production** → the list price of the **named in-production successor**, matched on cabin size, range, and mission. The comparable is stated so you can disagree with the choice.

Three cautions, stated up front:

1. **List price is a green airframe.** Interior completion, avionics options, and mission equipment are extra. On the government aircraft that gap is enormous — a [C-32A](/Planes/99-0004-Vance/overview) is a $76M Boeing before anyone installs a secure communications suite.
2. **Nobody pays list.** Fleet buyers negotiate. Treat these as ceilings for a single-unit retail purchase.
3. **Successor pricing cuts both ways.** A new G600 is more capable than the 1998 Gulfstream V it stands in for. The figure answers "what would it cost to buy this capability new today," not "what is this specific used aircraft worth."

Aircraft types and registration details come from the **FAA Civil Aviation Registry** — the complete releasable database, queried directly. Where this page and older commentary disagree on an aircraft type, **the registry wins**.

## What the numbers show

**The cheapest aircraft in the investigation is [N55906](/Planes/N55906/overview) at about $0.5 million** — a 1973 four-seat Piper Cherokee Arrow registered to a private individual in Winter Park, Florida. It is a single-engine piston trainer. It appears on this site because an investigator flagged it as a "second aircraft of interest" alongside the Army's ISR jet. A new one costs roughly **one one-hundred-and-sixteenth** of the Global 6500 it was listed beside. That mismatch is worth weighing when judging the claim.

**The most expensive is the [C-32A](/Planes/99-0004-Vance/overview) that carried Charlie Kirk's casket** — a Boeing 757-200 whose in-production equivalent, the BBJ MAX 7, lists at $76.5 million before a government VIP fit-out that would push it past $100 million.

**The Egyptian armada, priced as a fleet, is roughly $290 million of aircraft.** Five VIP jets of Gulfstream and Dassault class. That is a national-asset-scale deployment, whichever explanation for their movements turns out to be correct.

**The Army's ISR aircraft are not the expensive part of the Army's ISR programme.** A Global 6500 airframe lists at $56–58 million; the [HADES](/US_Intelligence/US_Army_HADES) sensor and mission fit-out that turns it into an intelligence platform is a separate government cost that dwarfs it and is not in these figures.
