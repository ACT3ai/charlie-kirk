## What is alleged

The thesis has two halves, and they have to be kept apart because they are not equally strong.

**The mechanism half.** The claim is that a shaped charge was positioned to fire through the
**center of the chest** — that the intended effect was to blow open the chest at the heart, so that
the injury would read to every observer as a rifle shot straight through the heart. On this
account the charge did not fire true: it **tilted**, and the jet went into the **neck** instead of
the sternum. Everything downstream follows from that error. The wound is in the wrong place for the
story that had already been prepared, and so the story and the body stop matching. This is the
weakest part of the thesis and it should be labelled clearly: no device has been recovered, no
autopsy has been released, and no test has been run. It sits alongside the other device readings
in [Chest-Shot Witnesses](/Mic/Chest_Shot_Witnesses) and [the shaped-charge demonstration](/Mic/Demo_of_shape_charge)
as an interpretation, not a finding.

**The narrative half.** This is the part that rests on something anyone can check. Across a large
number of direct-to-camera interviews, people appear to say they **personally saw** it — that they
watched him get shot straight through the heart, with the blood coming from the **center of his
chest**. Not "somewhere in the upper body." The heart, specifically, and the chest, specifically,
described in the first person. The observation offered here is that these accounts arrived as an
**initial wave**: they surfaced fast, they reached TV news in a number of different places, and
then — quickly — that framing was turned off. It appears to stop after a while. Later coverage
runs on a **neck** wound and largely stops repeating the heart language at all.

## What the shirt does to it

The reason the heart description is worth a page is that it is **checkable, and it fails the
check**. Charlie Kirk was wearing a **white shirt**. A round or a jet through the center of the
chest leaves that shirt torn at the entry and soaked from the middle outward, and both would be
plainly visible against white fabric on ordinary viewing. Neither is there. There is no chest
entry, no shredded shirt front, and no blood blooming from the sternum. The blood is at the
**neck**.

So the heart accounts are not a close call that the footage merely fails to confirm. They describe
something the footage shows did not happen. That is the whole engine of this page: the claim was
confident, it was specific, it was first-person, it was on television — and it was wrong in a way
a white shirt makes obvious to anyone who looks. The thesis asks why a wrong description arrived
that early and that uniformly.

## The featured clip, and what is new in it

The segment above is the same live TV-news interview already archived on this site in two shorter
cuts — via [@charlie_ward77](https://x.com/charlie_ward77/status/2076465128329846991) and
[@Maxwell1995Vjjo](https://x.com/Maxwell1995Vjjo/status/1966235480732873149), both about 52
seconds. This cut runs **3 minutes 43 seconds**, and two things appear in it that are in neither
short version.

**First, both witnesses are named on air.** The anchor asks "Your name?" and gets two answers:
**Angie Martin** and **Darren Brunson**. [Angie Martin](/People/angie-martin) is the
retired-law-enforcement witness — she says that "because of my past experience in law enforcement,
I knew it was a gun." **Darren Brunson** is the man who delivers the heart line. Every earlier page
on this site had to describe him as "the witness is not named in the clip." This cut supplies the
name.

**Second, the segment keeps going** past the point where the short cuts end, into Angie Martin's
account of the crowd and her closing remark to the anchor.

### Transcript — key statements

Auto-transcribed, lightly cleaned for readability, reproduced as spoken and attributed to the
speaker rather than adjudicated here.

**Darren Brunson, on the wound:**

> Yeah, I was there, and I watched the shooting. It looked like that, uh, he got hit, a **direct
> shot, right to the heart**. As soon as he was hit, he fell backwards, went to the side. The
> audience went to their knees. I stayed up a little bit because I wanted to see if anyone saw the
> shooting, if they would jump all over the guy. **I saw nothing like that.**

And later in the same answer:

> I think whoever did it was a good shooter, a sharpshooter. **It was one shot right directly to
> the heart**, and we need to find out who did this. I think there should be a research completely
> done that took place hours, maybe a day before anything went on. Who could be there and pull
> that trigger and do it?

**Angie Martin, on what she heard and did:**

> So I went towards the doors and heard a crack — because of my past experience in law
> enforcement, I knew it was a gun, and then I tucked behind the corner, and all the crowds
> started racing out… I was worried about people trampling each other. And I saw two college-age
> girls, one was having a panic attack. So I grabbed both of them because she was collapsing and
> rushed her down underneath the tunnel and got her to calm down, then put her in my car and got
> hold of her parents.

Three details in Brunson's answer matter beyond the heart phrase. He says he **deliberately stayed
standing** to see whether the crowd would tackle a shooter, and saw nothing of the kind. He is
asked twice whether the man taken into custody had **anything in his hands** — a device, anything
— and answers plainly: *"No, I did not."* That is a witness on live television declining to
supply the hand-device claim, which is relevant to [the Rick Cutler hand-device item](./cutler-hand-device)
and to the device readings generally. And he describes the detained man as calm and compliant.

### On the man taken into custody

Both witnesses discuss the man detained at the scene, whom Brunson names and says he believes was
**"the wrong guy."** That man — [George Zinn](/distraction_people/George_Zinn) — is a **living
person**. Authorities reported that he was **not the shooter**, and he was charged with counts
unrelated to the shooting. This site does not assert that he committed any crime in connection with
the assassination. The witnesses' characterisations of him are their own on-air opinions, recorded
here only because the "wrong guy" framing is inseparable from the rest of their statements, and
their further personal allegations about him are **not reproduced on this page**.

Angie Martin also closes the segment with an on-air impression about the person who asked the final
gun question — *"it almost felt like that the person that was asking the questions about gun knew
it was gonna happen."* That is a **witness impression and nothing more**. No evidence supports it,
it has never been substantiated, and the questioner — a living private citizen — is not accused of
anything by this site. It is recorded because the segment is being archived in full, not because
the site gives it weight.

## The ordinary explanation

This has to be given properly, because it explains the whole pattern without any device at all.

**Wound location is the single least reliable thing a witness reports.** Under acute stress, from
30 metres away, in a crowd that is already moving, with a sightline partly blocked by other
people, observers routinely place wounds wrong. "Shot in the heart" is not a forensic statement
when a bystander says it — it is **ordinary English for "shot fatally in the upper body."** People
reach for it precisely because it conveys *he is not going to survive this*, which was the one
thing every witness there did correctly perceive.

**Television selects for it.** Live news bookers and producers take the most vivid, most
confident, most quotable account available in the first hour. A witness who says "one shot right
directly to the heart" gets airtime; a witness who says "I'm not sure where he was hit" does not.
That is a **selection effect**, and it manufactures uniformity out of a mixed pool of accounts
without anybody being told what to say. It also explains the concentration on TV specifically, which
is the part of the pattern the thesis leans on hardest.

**And the fade has a boring cause.** The heart language receded exactly when better video and
official statements arrived. Coverage moved to the neck wound because the **evidence** moved there.
A narrative dying when it is contradicted is what is supposed to happen; it is not by itself
evidence that the narrative was installed. Note too that the heart accounts were never actually
unanimous — on the same day, other witnesses described blood "gushing out of his neck," which is
catalogued alongside the heart claims at [Shot in the Heart](/Narrative/Shot_in_the_heart).

What survives all of that is narrower than the thesis, and it is a fair question: the heart
accounts were unusually **specific** and unusually **first-person** — "I watched it," "right where
his heart is" — for a detail that a white shirt shows did not occur. Whether that exceeds ordinary
chaos and airtime selection is not settled by anything currently public.

## What would settle it

1. **Release the autopsy.** It describes the wound track. It would establish whether anything
   entered the chest at all, and would end the heart-versus-neck question outright.
2. **Pull the raw TV feeds and pre-interview notes.** If the heart phrasing was fed to witnesses,
   that shows up in the unaired tape and the booking notes, not in the broadcast.
3. **Interview the named witnesses directly.** Both [Angie Martin](/People/angie-martin) and Darren
   Brunson gave their names on air. Nobody has published a follow-up asking Brunson what he actually
   saw, from where, and whether he meant "heart" literally.
4. **Run the shaped-charge test.** A charge fired at a torso surrogate in a white shirt — on-axis
   and deliberately tilted — would show whether a tilted charge can produce this wound and this
   shirt. Until that is filmed, the mechanism half of the thesis stays SPECULATIVE.
5. **Count the accounts properly.** A systematic tally of every first-hour interview, coded for
   wound location and for whether the outlet was broadcast or citizen video, would test the
   "TV concentration" claim instead of asserting it.
